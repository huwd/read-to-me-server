The Quiet Engineering of Canals

By Ada Lovelock

Canals are among the oldest pieces of engineered infrastructure still in daily use. Their builders worked without modern surveying instruments, yet many of the routes they chose remain hard to improve on today. A canal has to hold water, and water only moves downhill, so every mile is a negotiation with the shape of the land.

The simplest answer is to follow a contour, winding around hills at a constant height. Contour canals are cheap to dig and need no locks, but they are long and slow. A boat might travel three miles to cover one as the crow flies, and every extra mile means more bank to maintain and more water lost to leaks.

Where a canal must climb, the engineer reaches for the lock: a chamber with gates at each end, filled or emptied to lift a boat from one level to the next. A single lock might raise a boat by eight or ten feet. Where the climb is steep, locks are built in flights, one after another up the hillside, so that the top gate of one chamber is the bottom gate of the next.

Every time a boat passes through a lock, a chamber full of water flows downhill and out of the summit. Keeping the summit supplied is the hidden problem of canal engineering. Builders dug reservoirs, diverted streams and installed pumping engines, and on a dry summer the shortage of water, not the state of the locks, decided whether boats could move at all.

Today most canals carry leisure boats rather than coal, but the same rules apply. The water still has to be found, the banks still have to hold, and a flight of locks still turns a hillside into a staircase that a boat can climb, one patient step at a time.
